# Rules of classical facade design

A catalogue of the rules that Renaissance and classicist architects followed when designing facades, with how binding each one was in practice.

Most of these rules come from a few treatises: Vitruvius (c. 25 BC), Alberti (1452), Serlio (1537), Vignola (1562), Palladio (1570), Scamozzi (1615) and Chambers (1759). They agree on how a facade is put together but disagree on the numbers. The Palladio, Alberti and Chambers rules below were checked against scans of their 18th-century English translations (see [Sources](#sources)).

Two common misconceptions:

- **Vitruvius's order proportions are neither exact nor complete.** He has no Composite order and leaves gaps elsewhere, and every Renaissance author published different numbers.
- **Vitruvius's *symmetria* does not mean mirror symmetry.** It means every part is a fraction or multiple of one module.

## Necessity levels

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hard** | Treated as almost never broken. Breaking it looks like an error, or like a deliberate Mannerist "licence". |
| **Canon** | Some value is required, but which one depends on the author. Pick one author and stay consistent. |
| **Thumb** | Rule of thumb, often with ranges, bent freely. |
| **Context** | Applies only to some building types, regions, periods, or when a given element is present. |
| **Optional** | A method or flourish, never required. |
| **Myth** | Attributed afterwards; no historical basis as a rule. |

## 1. The whole facade

- **Hard – mirror symmetry about a central axis** on the main front. Relaxed for side and rear fronts and awkward urban plots.
- **Hard – an opening on the axis, never a solid.** Alberti: supports (columns, corners) are always even in number and openings always odd, "as nature put the mouth single in the middle". Palladio: always an even number of columns, with the middle gap slightly wider.
- **Hard – openings stacked vertically.** Palladio: "the void over the void, and the solid upon the solid", with upper columns plumb over lower ones.
- **Hard – all windows in one storey are identical,** even if the rooms behind them differ (Palladio).
- **Thumb, strong – keep openings away from corners**, by at least one opening's width (Palladio). In arcades, his corner piers are ⅔ of the opening wide, against ⅓ for ordinary piers.
- **Thumb – split into three:** base / body / crown vertically (like pedestal / column / entablature), and often centre / wings horizontally.
- **Thumb/Context – emphasise the centre:** a wider middle bay (Vitruvius: 3 diameters there vs 2¼ elsewhere), the main door, a pediment, or a projecting centre.

## 2. The orders

Column height in lower diameters (D) — **Canon**:

| | Tuscan | Doric | Ionic | Corinthian | Composite |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vitruvius | 7 | 7 | 8–10 (varies with spacing) | ≈ Ionic + ⅔ (taller capital) | – |
| Serlio | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
| Vignola | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 10 |
| Palladio | 7 | 7½–8 | 9 | 9½ | 10 |
| Chambers | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 10 |

- **Canon – entablature (beam zone above the columns).** Vignola: ¼ of the column for every order, plus an optional pedestal of ⅓, giving pedestal : column : entablature = 4 : 12 : 3. Palladio: ¼ for Doric, ⅕ for Ionic, Corinthian and Composite. Scamozzi: ⅕.
- **Canon – wider spacing means stouter columns** (Vitruvius, Palladio):
  - more than 3 D apart → 7 D tall
  - 3 D → 7½–8
  - 2¼ D → 9
  - 2 D → 9½
  - 1½ D → 10
- **Canon for free-standing colonnades, Thumb for pilasters on a wall – named spacings:** 1½, 2, 2¼, 3 D, and over 3 D only with timber beams. On a wall, the window usually sets the bay width instead.
- **Hard within Doric – a triglyph (the grooved frieze block) centred over every column, with square panels (metopes) between.** This makes Doric bay widths discrete: in Vignola, column spacing must be a multiple of 1¼ D. It is the strongest discrete constraint on a bay grid.
  - The same idea for modillions (cornice brackets) and dentils is only **Thumb**; Chambers accepts near-misses.
- **Context – arches belong on piers, columns carry straight beams** (Alberti). Widely ignored in loggias. Arch openings are about 2 : 1 tall (Vignola).
- **Context – which order suits which building.** Vitruvius ties orders to gods (Doric to Mars, Corinthian to Venus). Serlio maps them to saints and building types, with rustic Tuscan for gates and fortresses.

## 3. Stacking orders

- **Hard – strongest at the bottom:** Tuscan → Doric → Ionic → Corinthian → Composite, never inverted.
  - Scamozzi and Chambers put Composite *below* Corinthian, so the top order depends on the author.
  - Palladio explicitly allows skipping an order. Chambers calls skipping or repeating an order "not regular", though the Colosseum repeats one.
- **Hard – column axes aligned** between storeys. Chambers allows only a slight step back, as at the Theatre of Marcellus.
- **Upper order smaller – Hard in direction, Canon in amount.**
  - Vitruvius: each tier ¼ shorter. Chambers shows this becomes absurd by the third tier.
  - Scamozzi, "universally esteemed the best" (Chambers): the upper column's bottom diameter equals the lower column's top diameter, "as if … one long tapering tree, cut into several pieces".
  - With the usual ⅙ taper, an Ionic storey over a Doric one comes out 15/16 as tall, so storeys are nearly equal.
- **Thumb – at most two tiers of orders on one front** (Chambers). Italian buildings often have three.
- **Optional alternatives:** a giant order spanning two storeys (Michelangelo's Capitoline palaces, Palladio's Palazzo Valmarana), or a rusticated basement + one order + an attic.

## 4. Storeys

- **Thumb, strong – order of importance:** base → *piano nobile* (main floor; tallest, richest) → lower, plainer upper floors → attic or mezzanine (smallest).
- **Thumb – Palladio's ⅙:** second-storey rooms are ⅙ lower than those below. A flat-ceilinged room is as high as it is wide. Vaulted rooms use the arithmetic, geometric or harmonic mean of length and width.
- **Hard, as a consequence – with an order on a storey,** storey height = (pedestal +) column + entablature. Choosing the storey height fixes the module.
- **Thumb – basement** (rusticated ground floor carrying the order), per Chambers:
  - between ½ and 1× the order's height, never taller than the order;
  - at least ½ if it contains rooms;
  - each rustication course about 1 module high.
- **Thumb – attic:** ¼ to ⅓ of the order below it, shaped like a pedestal (Chambers).
- **Context – regional types:**
  - Florence: storeys decreasing upward, rough-to-smooth stonework, no orders.
  - Rome: smooth walls, corner stones, framed windows.
  - England: basement / main floor / attic.

## 5. Windows and doors

- **Thumb, strong – same width on every storey, height shrinking upward.** Palladio: each storey's windows ⅙ shorter than those below. Chambers' height : width ratios:
  - ground floor: up to 2
  - main floor: 2⅛ to 2⅓
  - second floor: 1½ to 1⅘
  - attic: 1 or less
- **Thumb – window size:**
  - Palladio: width ¼ to ⅕ of the room's width, height 2⅙ × width. His example: an 18 × 30 ft room gets a 4 ft window.
  - Chambers: width = (room depth + room height) / 8.
  - Chambers, relative to the order: about 3 modules wide if the order spans two storeys, 4½–5 modules if one.
- **Thumb – sills level across a storey**, 2′9″ to 3′ above the floor (Chambers). A fixed body-related size that does not scale with the building.
- **Thumb – frames** ⅙ to ⅕ of the opening's width (Palladio).
- **Thumb – ornament hierarchy:** pediments on the main floor (Palazzo Farnese alternates triangular and curved), a moulded frame higher up, a plain frame in the attic.
- **Thumb – doors:** the main door is about 1 : 2.4 (Palladio after Vitruvius). Room doors have fixed sizes: 2–3 ft wide, 5–6½ ft high.
- The treatises give no fixed width for the wall between windows. It follows from the bay grid; only corners have a minimum.

## 6. Cornice, pediment, roofline

- **Thumb – on a facade without orders, the top cornice is sized to the whole front,** not the top storey (Florentine palaces: Medici, Strozzi, Farnese). Chambers' figures, from Vignola and Gibbs, as a fraction of the front's height:
  - rich full entablature: 1/11
  - simpler entablatures: 1/13–1/15
  - cornice alone: 1/16–1/18
- **Optional – a cornice of ⅛ of the building height**, by analogy with the head being ⅛ of the body. The head proportion is genuine Vitruvius, and Francesco di Giorgio drew heads into cornice profiles, but no treatise states this as a rule, and it is heavier than every codified value. The only ⅛ in Chambers is a reduced entablature measured against a *column*, not a building.
- **Canon – pediment pitch:**
  - Vitruvius: height 1/9 of the width, about 12.5°.
  - Serlio's compass construction: about 22.5°.
  - Palladio's roofs: 2/9 of the span, about 24°.
  - Chambers: height ⅕–¼ of the base, about 22–27°, steeper for small pediments and flatter for large ones. Pitch depends on size.
- **Hard in academic classicism, broken in the Baroque – a pediment is a roof:**
  - one per composition, at the top;
  - never stacked, nested or broken (Palladio calls broken pediments "the most important error");
  - curved pediments only over small elements.
- **Thumb – a cornice projects about as far as it is tall.** Palladio lists over-projecting cornices among his "abuses".

## 7. Proportion systems

- **Thumb, strong – small whole-number ratios.** This, not special geometry, is the real Renaissance practice.
- **Thumb – Palladio's seven room shapes:** circle, 1:1, √2:1, 4:3, 3:2, 5:3, 2:1. Serlio gives a similar list.
- **Optional/Context – musical ratios** (Alberti): ratios that please the ear please the eye, e.g. 1:1, 2:3, 3:4, 1:2, 4:9, 9:16, 1:3, 3:8, 1:4. Wittkower (1949) made the idea famous. How far it was really applied is debated; Palladio's printed plans are tidier than his built villas.
- **Myth – golden ratio.** No Renaissance architecture treatise prescribes it.
  - Pacioli's *De divina proportione* (1509) praises it mathematically, but follows Vitruvius's whole-number ratios for buildings.
  - The famous "golden" buildings are 19th–20th-century readings (Zeising, Ghyka, Le Corbusier).
  - They are easy to "find" because 3:2, 5:3 and 8:5 all sit close to φ. For the look, use 5:3: it is on Palladio's list.
- **Optional – human-body proportions** (Vitruvius III.1: body = 8 heads; Doric from a man's proportions, Ionic from a woman's). This is the *justification* for the order proportions, not a separate set of facade rules. The body measures that actually bind are the fixed ones: sills, doors, steps.
- **Optional – regulating lines** (parallel diagonals of similar rectangles): mostly a 20th-century way of analysing facades.

## 8. Optics and scale

- **Context – Vitruvius's optical corrections:** enlarge parts placed high, thicken corner columns, taper shafts with a slight swelling (entasis). Matters only at large scale.
- **Thumb – scaled vs. fixed elements:** things people only look at (orders, cornices) scale with the module. Things people touch (sills, doors, steps, balustrades) keep body-sized dimensions.

## 9. What counts as Hard depends on the school

Mannerists broke Palladio's rules on purpose, and the Baroque gave up several of them. Neoclassicists went stricter: Laugier (1753) rejected even pilasters, pedestals, arcades and attics. A target style (e.g. Palladian, or academic Vignola) determines which rules are Hard.

## Implications for a generative algorithm

- **Map the levels to mechanisms:**
  - Hard → constraints
  - Canon → one global choice of author (Vignola is easiest to compute)
  - Thumb → ranges or a score
  - Context → style flags
  - Optional/Myth → toggles
- **Subdivide around the axis, not by halving.** Splitting a facade at its midpoint puts a dividing line, i.e. a pier, exactly on the axis, the one place the rules forbid a solid. Classical subdivision is an odd number of bays around the axis (all equal, or a wide centre between narrower sides).
- **Superposition in module terms:** with Scamozzi's rule, the upper order's lower diameter is the lower order's upper diameter.
- **Prior work on generating classical designs:**
  - Stiny & Mitchell, "The Palladian grammar" (1978), a shape grammar;
  - Hersey & Freedman, *Possible Palladian Villas* (1992), computer-generated villas;
  - Tzonis & Lefaivre, *Classical Architecture: The Poetics of Order* (1986), which formalises grid and tripartition, orders, and rhythm;
  - Durand, *Précis des leçons d'architecture* (1802–05), grid-based composition.
